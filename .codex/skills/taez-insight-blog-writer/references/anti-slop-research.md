# Anti-Slop Research Notes

Use this only when a post concerns AI slop, workslop, AI-assisted writing, developer content saturation, or the difference between generic AI output and valuable AI-assisted content.

## Current Signal

- HBR/BetterUp/Stanford define workslop as AI-generated work that looks good but lacks substance to advance the task. Their 2025 survey of 1,150 US full-time employees reported that 40% had received workslop in the past month; resolving an incident took about 1 hour 56 minutes on average and created trust costs.
- BetterUp summarizes the pattern as AI work losing context and accountability: individuals feel frustrated, teams waste cycles, and organizations mistake activity for progress.
- Baltes, Cheong, and Treude's 2026 arXiv paper on AI slop in software development frames AI slop as low-quality AI-generated code, PRs, docs, and bug reports that creates review friction, degrades knowledge resources, erodes trust, and externalizes individual productivity costs onto reviewers and maintainers.
- Sergeyuk et al. 2026 longitudinal IDE study found AI users produced substantially more code but also deleted more, suggesting workflow restructuring rather than simple productivity increase.
- Graham Dumpleton's 2026 developer-advocacy post makes a useful distinction: AI-generated content is not automatically slop; content without intent, structure, domain expertise, audience design, or review is slop.

## Editorial Takeaways

- Do not define slop by tool origin. Define it by missing authorial judgment.
- Valuable AI-assisted writing has: intended reader, specific job-to-be-done, author's field evidence, clear constraints, and accountable review.
- Generic AI writing usually lacks: lived scene, tradeoff, failed attempt, local vocabulary, named artifact, or falsifiable claim.
- The best anti-slop move for TaeZ is not more personality. It is more traceable judgment: "I tried X, it worked until Y, then I changed my model to Z."

## Reusable Test

Ask these before publishing:

1. What sentence could only TaeZ write because it comes from his own practice?
2. What claim would a strong engineer object to, and how does the article survive that objection?
3. What does the reader now know how to do differently?
4. What artifact, workflow, failure, metric, or quote grounds the claim?
5. Did AI help produce text, or did it help expose and refine the author's own judgment?
